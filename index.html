Bench apple-design, under test

Evaluation rig · 8 benches

Does the advice survive contact with a finger?

The apple-design skill makes claims that are physical and testable — spring constants, a deceleration rate, a projection formula, a rule about which value an interruption starts from. Each bench below runs one claim as working code, next to the version that ignores it, with the live numbers on screen. Drag things. Flick them. Grab them mid-flight. The point is to feel the difference, or to find out there isn't one.

1:1 tracking, and the grab offset

Small rule, large effect

Momentum projection

Where flicks come from
Don’t snap to the nearest boundary from the release point. Use velocity to project the resting position, then snap to the target nearest that projected point. project(v) = (v/1000)·d/(1−d), d ≈ 0.998. §6 Momentum projection
deceleration rate0.998

Flick gently and hard, and watch the two markers separate. With projection off, a hard flick and a slow nudge land in the same place — the gesture’s energy is thrown away, which is exactly why some carousels feel deaf. The formula matters: the textbook v²/(2a) is not what ships, and at high velocity it overshoots badly. Drag the deceleration rate down to 0.99 for a snappier, shorter throw.

Two parameters instead of three

Useful, with one caveat
Damping ratio controls overshoot; response is how quickly the value reaches the target, in seconds. Start most UI at damping 1.0. Add bounce only when the gesture itself carried momentum. §4 Behavior over animation

The two knobs are genuinely easier to reason about than mass/stiffness/damping, and the presets land where the skill says they land. The “response is not a duration” warning turns out to be narrower than it sounds. Measured here: damping 1.0 / response 0.40 settles at 0.43 s, and damping 0.8 / response 0.40 settles at 0.40 s — for anything critically damped, response is a fair proxy for settle time. It only comes apart once you go bouncy: drag damping down to 0.3 and the same 0.40 s response is still ringing at 0.91 s, more than twice as long. That is a useful qualification the file doesn’t make.

Rubber-banding at a boundary

Cheap, and it works
At an edge, resist progressively instead of stopping hard. A hard stop reads as “frozen”; continuous resistance reads as “responsive, but there’s nothing more here.” §9 Rubber-banding

Tracking is size-specific

True, but the file undersells it
Large display text wants negative tracking; small text wants slightly positive tracking for legibility. A fixed letter-spacing is wrong somewhere. §15 Typography

The rule is correct and the difference is real at display size — the flat setting looks loose and slightly amateur. But this is the weakest bench on the page, and that is a fair finding: the effect is far smaller than the motion rules, it is nearly invisible at body size, and the skill gives one number for “large” rather than a scale. Judge it against the effort it costs, which is close to none.

What this page actually showed

  • The physics is real and correctly specified. The projection function, the rubber-band function and the damping/response mapping were used here verbatim, with no tuning, and they produce the behaviour the file promises. That is unusual for design writing, which normally stops at adjectives.
  • Interruptibility is the load-bearing idea. Bench 3 and bench 7 are the ones you can feel with your eyes closed. If the skill only ever changed one thing about a codebase, this would be worth it on its own.
  • Its ranking is off. Sixteen numbered sections read as sixteen equal rules, but the gap between §3 and §15 in perceived quality is enormous. A reader following it top to bottom will spend effort on letter-spacing before they fix a locked-out transition.
  • The visual sections are the thinner half. §12 and §15 are sound but generic — they are the parts that could have been written without the WWDC talks, and they give values without a scale. The motion sections are where the specific, hard-won detail is.
  • It assumes a spring library and then mostly doesn’t need one. Everything on this page is about 120 lines of dependency-free code. The file points at Motion / Framer Motion, which is fine, but it slightly undersells how reachable this is by hand.
  • Nothing in it measured as wrong. The closest thing to a correction is that its “response is not a duration” warning is broader than the data: at damping 0.8–1.0 response predicts settle time within about 8%. The warning only really applies below damping ~0.5, which is worth knowing, because it means you can budget timings with it after all.
